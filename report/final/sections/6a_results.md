{{table:final}} compares the final models. On test, the MLP has the highest macro-F1 ({{final:mlp:test_macro_f1}}) and accuracy ({{final:mlp:test_acc}}), followed by the 1D-CNN ({{final:cnn1d:test_macro_f1}}). The BiLSTM ({{final:bilstm:test_macro_f1}}) and its GRU ablation ({{final:gru:test_macro_f1}}) are within each other's spread, and the Transformer is last ({{final:transformer:test_macro_f1}}). On validation the order differs: the 1D-CNN ({{final:cnn1d:val_macro_f1}}) and the GRU ({{final:gru:val_macro_f1}}) lead, and the MLP ({{final:mlp:val_macro_f1}}) is near the bottom.

Every model scores lower on test than on validation, but the drop is smallest for the MLP and largest for the GRU and the Transformer. The raw-signal networks learn their features from 15 training subjects; they score as well as or better than the MLP on the six validation subjects but transfer less to nine new people. The 561 features instead carry prior knowledge of the signals, including the angles between gravity and the phone's axes, which plausibly separate the two static postures more directly than raw accelerations do. The summed test confusion matrices agree: the MLP confuses sitting and standing far less often than any raw-signal model, while the 1D-CNN is the strongest on the three walking classes (Section VI-B).

Among the raw-signal models the 1D-CNN transfers best. Its filters span 0.72 s and are shared across the window, a strong assumption that suits short repeating patterns and leaves little room to fit subject-specific detail. The recurrent models and the Transformer can relate any two time steps, which is more flexible but must be learned from the same 15 subjects; here it shows as a larger validation-to-test drop, not as higher accuracy. The Transformer, with the weakest built-in assumption about local structure, is last on test and slowest to train ({{final:transformer:train_time_s}} s). {{fig:final_val_curves}} shows the raw-signal models reaching their validation plateau within a few epochs, while the MLP improves more slowly.

Tuning ({{table:tuning}}) mattered most for the MLP, whose four configurations spread most widely on validation; all four 1D-CNN configurations tied within 0.001, and the BiLSTM kept its default. Seed variance is larger for the raw-signal models: the test macro-F1 standard deviation is {{final:mlp:test_macro_f1.std}} for the MLP and between {{final:cnn1d:test_macro_f1.std}} and {{final:gru:test_macro_f1.std}} for the others. The 1D-CNN's validation score hardly changes across seeds while its test score does, so the six validation subjects cannot separate good seeds from weaker ones, and small differences between models should be read against this spread.

{{fig:cost_tradeoff}} relates accuracy to cost (Objective 2). The MLP is also the fastest ({{final:mlp:cpu_latency_bs1_ms}} ms per window on one CPU thread), but this excludes computing its 561 features, which the dataset supplies but a device would compute for every window. Among the raw-signal models the 1D-CNN is both the most accurate and the fastest ({{final:cnn1d:cpu_latency_bs1_ms}} ms, against {{final:bilstm:cpu_latency_bs1_ms}} ms for the BiLSTM and {{final:transformer:cpu_latency_bs1_ms}} ms for the Transformer). Parameter count is a poor guide to cost: the GRU has the fewest parameters ({{final:gru:params}}) but the highest latency ({{final:gru:cpu_latency_bs1_ms}} ms). For on-device use without a feature extractor the 1D-CNN offers the best balance of accuracy and cost; where the features are available, the MLP is preferable.
